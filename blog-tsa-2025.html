Colorado TSA 2025–2026

What I learned and accomplished during my first time in the Colorado Technology Student Association.

February 19, 2026
5 min read
TSA
Arnav Jain

What Is TSA?

The Technology Student Association (TSA) is a national organization for students in STEM, where you compete in tech-related events against other schools. Colorado has its own chapter, and this year was my first time participating. I had no idea what to expect going in.

Events range from coding challenges and cybersecurity to video game design and engineering. You pick your events, prepare, and then compete at the regional and state levels. It's genuinely one of the best ways to apply what you're learning in a real competitive setting.

TSA has over 250,000 members across the US. Colorado's state conference brings together schools from all over the state to compete across 60+ events.

Events I Competed In

I signed up for a few events that lined up with what I've been working on. Here's what I competed in and how it went:

Robotics

This was the event I was most excited about and also the most nervous for. In this competition, participants must design and build a robot and carefully refine its performance. Once the robot is ready, it must be driven through a course with specific obstacles during the competition.

The course required the robot to navigate several obstacles, including picking up objects and driving up ramps. One of the most difficult sections involved using the robot’s arm to lift pipes. The robot had to slide the pipe upward, drive through it, and repeat this process multiple times. Later in the course, the robot also had to pick up eggs before returning to move the pipes again.

Our team was not able to complete the pipe section successfully because the robot’s arm struggled to grip the pipe. However, we were able to complete the rest of the course, which required precise control, coordination, and careful decision-making.

“Engineering is not about getting it right the first time. It is about improving it until it works.” — A lesson I learned while preparing for the robotics competition

Senior Solar Sprint

Tip for future competitors: Know your basic Linux commands cold. grep, find, chmod, netstat — these come up constantly in security challenges.

Results & Placement

I placed in my events at the regional level, which qualified me to move on to the state conference. State was a completely different level of competition — the preparation required and the quality of other competitors was noticeably higher.

I didn't take first at state, but I walked away with a much clearer picture of what the best competitors do differently. Their code is tighter, their explanations are cleaner, and they've clearly competed in these formats before. That's just experience — and now I have some too.

What I Actually Learned

Beyond the placements, here's what TSA actually taught me that I couldn't have learned from just building projects at home:

  • Time pressure changes everything. Code that you'd write in 2 hours normally has to happen in 30 minutes. Your instincts and muscle memory matter more than you'd think.
  • Explaining your work matters. Several events have a presentation component. Being able to clearly explain what you built and why is a skill that's separate from the technical work itself.
  • Competition calibrates you. You find out fast what gaps you have. That's uncomfortable but genuinely useful.
  • Teammates make or break team events. Communication under pressure is hard. Having a plan before you walk in the door is non-negotiable.

What's Next

I'm planning to compete again next year with more preparation and a clearer strategy. There are events I didn't try this year — video game design and data science especially — that I want to take on.

If you're thinking about joining TSA and you're into tech at all, just do it. The worst case is you lose and learn something. The best case is you win and still learn something.
