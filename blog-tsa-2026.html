Prepared for my second year competing in Colorado TSA — with better plans, bigger events, and lessons from year one.

Year Two

After finishing my first year of TSA, I learned a lot about what it actually takes to compete at a high level. This post covers what I am planning for year two, what events I am entering, what I am doing differently, and how I am approaching each one with the lessons from 2025–2026 in mind.

The biggest difference between year one and year two is preparation. Last year I started late, prepared narrowly, and ran out of time on multiple builds. This year, the plan is built around avoiding every one of those mistakes.

TSA has over 250,000 members across the US. Colorado's state conference brings together schools from all over the state to compete across 60+ events.

Events I Am Competing In

This year I chose events that match what I have been building on since last season, with one new challenge added:

Flight Endurance HS

Difficulty: ★★★★☆

In Flight Endurance, teams design and build a rubber-band-powered balsa wood airplane that must stay airborne as long as possible. The plane is judged entirely on flight time, so the design, weight distribution, and power system have to work together perfectly.

This is the event I am most focused on this year. After working with motors and mechanical systems in Robotics last year, I want to apply that precision thinking to an aerodynamics challenge. The learning curve is steep, but the design problem is exactly the kind I enjoy working through.

Key focus: Weight is everything. Even a small amount of extra material can significantly reduce flight time. Every component needs a reason to be there.

Video Game Design

Difficulty: ★★★☆☆

Teams design and build an original video game using C#, then present it to judges. The judging criteria covers game design, playability, originality, and the quality of the team's presentation about their development process.

This event is a good fit because I enjoy game logic and building interactive systems. My plan is to keep the scope tight — a smaller, polished game will score better than an ambitious one that is half-finished.

"Failure is data. Every mistake tells you exactly what to fix next." — What I took away from last year's Solar Sprint gear ratio problem

Robotics

Difficulty: ★★★☆☆

Last year, we didn't place in the top three in this event because we weren't able to pick up the pipe and weren't professional enough during the interview.

The robot had to complete a timed obstacle course, by manual control depending on the event format. There was a lot of room for things to go wrong, which is exactly why early testing mattered so much.

Improvement for this year: We built and redesigned the robot, giving it over three months to test instead of rushing at the last minute.

What I Expect on Competition Day

After going through it once, I know what to expect. Walking in, there is a lot of energy. Teams are testing builds, checking equipment, and running through last-minute practice. Last year that environment caught me off guard. This year I am planning for it.

The biggest thing I learned from competition day last year is that anything that can go wrong will go wrong at the worst possible moment. The robot arm that worked perfectly at home will suddenly struggle under pressure. The best thing you can do is practice under pressure conditions long before competition day arrives.

Mistakes from Year One

Before planning year two, I wrote down every mistake I made in year one. Here is the honest list:

Gear ratio too large for Solar Sprint. We built a well-designed car but never tested whether the motor had enough torque to move it. A single bench test before competition would have caught it.
Did not order drone parts early enough. We knew about the Drone Challenge weeks in advance. Still ran out of time. Large builds need a hard ordering deadline, not a rough intention.
Only studied Python for the Coding event. The exam covered multiple languages. One language was never enough. We should have known this going in.
Did not practice the robot arm enough. The pipe-gripping section was the hardest obstacle and we had not drilled it nearly enough before showing up.

Next Year Strategy

Rather than just a general goal, here is the specific action plan for year two:

1
Start the drone build in November — parts ordered before Thanksgiving, frame assembled by December.
2
Test the robot arm specifically. Run it through the pipe section 20+ times before competition day.
3
Study Java and C++ basics alongside Python for Coding HS. Two sessions per week starting January.
4
For Flight Endurance, test at least three different wing configurations before settling on a final design.
5
Keep the Video Game Design scope tight. A polished small game beats an ambitious unfinished one.

What's Next

This post will be updated as the season progresses — with photos, results, and what actually happened versus what was planned. That gap between plan and reality is usually where the most interesting lessons come from.

Year one taught me that TSA is genuinely hard. The competitors who place well are prepared in ways that are not obvious until you are standing next to them on competition day. Year two is about closing that gap.

The goal is not just to place higher — it is to leave knowing I prepared as well as I possibly could. Everything else follows from that.
